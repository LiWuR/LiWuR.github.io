Intertemporal Equilibrium with Production and Endogenous Interest Rates

The \(3\times2\) Model

Following the structural-equilibrium treatment of money and interest (Li, 2019, chap. 7), this example considers a finite-horizon intertemporal equilibrium with production and endogenous interest rates. Money is treated as an intertemporal asset. One unit of money supplies one unit of money service in each period, and the value of that service is the interest generated by the money in that period. Accordingly, there are three types of dated commodities—product, labor, and money service—and two types of economic agents—firm and consumer.

Assume the following:

  1. The economy operates for \(n_p\geq3\) periods, indexed by \(1,\ldots,n_p\). Production takes place in periods \(i=1,\ldots,n_p-1\). Production in period \(i\) produces product \(i+1\), which is available in period \(i+1\). Accordingly, the dated products are product \(2,\ldots,n_p\), while the dated labor and money-service commodities are labor \(1,\ldots,n_p-1\) and money service \(1,\ldots,n_p-1\). There is no product 1 commodity in the model; the first product generated by production is product 2. Thus, there are \(3(n_p-1)\) dated commodities. There is one firm activity in each of the first \(n_p-1\) periods and one consumer. Product 2 is the numeraire.
  2. In production period \(i\), the firm uses labor \(l^{(i)}\) to produce product \(i+1\) according to

\[ y^{(i)}=\alpha^{(i)} l^{(i)}, \qquad \alpha^{(i)}>0 \]

Let \(y^{(i)}\) also denote the firm’s activity level, measured by output. Thus, producing \(y^{(i)}\) units of product \(i+1\) requires \(y^{(i)}/\alpha^{(i)}\) units of labor \(i\). Before production, the firm presells product \(i+1\) and must hold money while purchasing labor \(i\). Because one unit of money held in period \(i\) supplies one unit of money service in that period, the firm’s money holdings are also its demand for money service \(i\).

  1. The consumer has the intertemporal Cobb–Douglas utility function

\[ u=\prod_{i=1}^{n_p-1}\left(c^{(i+1)}\right)^{\beta^{(i)}} \]

where \(c^{(i+1)}\) denotes consumption of product \(i+1\), produced in period \(i\). The expenditure shares are strictly decreasing over production periods,

\[ \beta^{(1)}>\beta^{(2)}>\cdots>\beta^{(n_p-1)}>0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n_p-1}\beta^{(i)}=1 \]

The consumer is endowed with \(\omega^{(i)}>0\) units of labor \(i\), \(i=1,\ldots,n_p-1\). The decreasing-share assumption will ensure strictly positive equilibrium interest rates in all nonterminal production periods. 4. The consumer owns one unit of money. This unit supplies one unit of money service in each production period \(i=1,\ldots,n_p-1\). Let \(p_m^{(i)}\) denote the interest generated by one unit of money in period \(i\). We restrict attention to equilibria with strictly positive nonterminal interest rates,

\[ r^{(i)}>0, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

while the terminal condition is

\[ r^{(n_p-1)}=+\infty \]

For the terminal production period, define the finite interest-inclusive product price

\[ \tilde p^{(n_p)} := \lim_{r^{(n_p-1)}\rightarrow+\infty} (1+r^{(n_p-1)})p^{(n_p)} \]

This variable allows the terminal demands to be written directly without introducing an infinite interest rate into the equilibrium system.

For the four-period case, \(n_p=4\), there are three production periods. Let

\[ \mathbf p = \left( 1,p^{(3)},p^{(4)}, p_l^{(1)},p_l^{(2)},p_l^{(3)}, p_m^{(1)},p_m^{(2)},p_m^{(3)} \right)^\top \]

where the last three components are the interests generated by one unit of money in periods 1–3. Let \(\mathbf y=(y^{(1)},y^{(2)},y^{(3)})^\top\) denote firm activity levels, and let \(w\) denote consumer income. The consumer’s income is the value of the labor endowments plus the interests generated by the consumer’s unit of money,

\[ w = \sum_{i=1}^3p_l^{(i)}\omega^{(i)} + \sum_{i=1}^3p_m^{(i)} \]

With rows representing product 2–4, labor 1–3, and money service 1–3, and columns representing the three firms and the consumer, respectively, the demand matrix is

\[ \mathbf D(\mathbf p,\mathbf y,w,r^{(1)},r^{(2)},\tilde p^{(4)}) = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&0&\dfrac{\beta^{(1)}w}{1+r^{(1)}}\\[6pt] 0&0&0&\dfrac{\beta^{(2)}w}{(1+r^{(2)})p^{(3)}}\\[6pt] 0&0&0&\dfrac{\beta^{(3)}w}{\tilde p^{(4)}}\\[6pt] \dfrac{y^{(1)}}{\alpha^{(1)}}&0&0&0\\[6pt] 0&\dfrac{y^{(2)}}{\alpha^{(2)}}&0&0\\[6pt] 0&0&\dfrac{y^{(3)}}{\alpha^{(3)}}&0\\[6pt] \dfrac{r^{(1)}p_l^{(1)}y^{(1)}}{\alpha^{(1)}p_m^{(1)}}&0&0& \dfrac{\beta^{(1)}wr^{(1)}}{(1+r^{(1)})p_m^{(1)}}\\[8pt] 0&\dfrac{r^{(2)}p_l^{(2)}y^{(2)}}{\alpha^{(2)}p_m^{(2)}}&0& \dfrac{\beta^{(2)}wr^{(2)}}{(1+r^{(2)})p_m^{(2)}}\\[8pt] 0&0&0&\dfrac{\beta^{(3)}w}{p_m^{(3)}} \end{bmatrix} \]

and the supply matrix is

\[ \mathbf S(\mathbf y) = \begin{bmatrix} y^{(1)}&0&0&0\\ 0&y^{(2)}&0&0\\ 0&0&y^{(3)}&0\\ 0&0&0&\omega^{(1)}\\ 0&0&0&\omega^{(2)}\\ 0&0&0&\omega^{(3)}\\ 0&0&0&1\\ 0&0&0&1\\ 0&0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The corresponding equality-form wide-sense structural equilibrium model is

\[ \mathbf p^\top\mathbf D = \mathbf p^\top\mathbf S \]

\[ \mathbf D\mathbf 1 = \mathbf S\mathbf 1 \]

The third firm’s zero demand for money service is a terminal-limit result rather than an additional assumption. As the terminal interest rate tends to infinity, the terminal product price and the third-period labor price both tend to zero in a way that makes the firm’s money holdings, and hence its demand for money service, vanish. Under the same terminal-limit treatment, the consumer’s terminal-period product demand and money holdings are written directly in terms of \(\tilde p^{(4)}\) and \(p_m^{(3)}\).

For each nonterminal production period, labor- and product-market clearing, together with the firm’s revenue-expenditure balance condition, determine the firm’s equilibrium money holdings. Combining the firm’s and consumer’s holdings with money-service market clearing gives the equilibrium interest

\[ p_m^{(i)*} = \beta^{(i)}w^* \frac{ r^{(i)*}(r^{(i)*}+2) }{ (1+r^{(i)*})^2 }, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

Under the terminal condition, the terminal firm’s money holdings vanish, while the consumer holds the entire unit of money in the terminal production period. Hence, the terminal interest is

\[ p_m^{(n_p-1)*} = \beta^{(n_p-1)}w^* \]

These interest relations provide the basis for deriving the backward recursion for the endogenous equilibrium interest rates.

Analytical Solution for the Endogenous Interest Rates

The endogenous interest rates are determined by the equilibrium money values. Define the money value in production period \(i\) as the value of all remaining interests generated from period \(i\) onward:

\[ v^{(i)} := \sum_{j=i}^{n_p-1}p_m^{(j)*} \]

Hence,

\[ v^{(i)}=p_m^{(i)*}+v^{(i+1)} \]

That is, the current money value equals the current interest plus the continuation value of money. For each nonterminal period, the equilibrium interest rate is therefore

\[ r^{(i)*} = \frac{p_m^{(i)*}}{v^{(i+1)}}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

The denominator is \(v^{(i+1)}\) rather than \(v^{(i)}\) because \(v^{(i+1)}\) is the principal value that remains after the current interest has been paid, whereas \(v^{(i)}\) already includes that interest. Consequently,

\[ 1+r^{(i)*} = \frac{v^{(i)}}{v^{(i+1)}} \]

Normalize the money value by consumer income:

\[ g^{(i)} := \frac{v^{(i)}}{w^*} \]

The terminal value is

\[ g^{(n_p-1)}=\beta^{(n_p-1)} \]

and

\[ 1+r^{(i)*} = \frac{g^{(i)}}{g^{(i+1)}} \]

Using

\[ \frac{p_m^{(i)*}}{w^*} = r^{(i)*}g^{(i+1)} \]

together with the money-service market-clearing expression gives

\[ \left(g^{(i)}\right)^2-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i)}-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i+1)}=0 \]

Thus, the positive root yields the backward recursion

\[ g^{(i)} = \frac{ \beta^{(i)}+ \sqrt{ \left(\beta^{(i)}\right)^2+4\beta^{(i)} g^{(i+1)} } }{2}, \qquad i=n_p-2,\ldots,1 \]

with

\[ g^{(n_p-1)}=\beta^{(n_p-1)} \]

Once the sequence \(g^{(i)}\) is obtained, the nonterminal equilibrium interest rates follow from

\[ r^{(i)*} = \frac{g^{(i)}}{g^{(i+1)}}-1, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

The decreasing-share assumption guarantees that these interest rates are strictly positive. To see this, note first that

\[ g^{(n_p-1)}=\beta^{(n_p-1)}<2\beta^{(n_p-1)} \]

Suppose \(g^{(i+1)}<2\beta^{(i+1)}\). Since \(\beta^{(i)}>\beta^{(i+1)}\),

\[ g^{(i+1)}<2\beta^{(i)} \]

Using

\[ \left(g^{(i)}\right)^2-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i)}-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i+1)}=0 \]

and evaluating the left-hand side at \(g^{(i+1)}\) and \(2\beta^{(i)}\) shows that the positive root satisfies

\[ g^{(i+1)}<g^{(i)}<2\beta^{(i)} \]

This establishes the induction step. Proceeding backward therefore gives

\[ g^{(1)}>g^{(2)}>\cdots>g^{(n_p-1)}>0 \]

and hence

\[ r^{(i)*}>0, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

Thus, for any finite \(n_p\), the backward recursion directly determines the strictly positive nonterminal equilibrium interest rates under the maintained assumption \(\beta^{(1)}>\beta^{(2)}>\cdots>\beta^{(n_p-1)}>0\). The productivity parameters \(\alpha^{(i)}\) and labor endowments \(\omega^{(i)}\) affect equilibrium quantities and prices but do not enter the interest-rate recursion.

Common Nonterminal Interest Rate

A useful special case imposes a common nonterminal equilibrium interest rate,

\[ r^{(i)*}=r>0, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

Then

\[ g^{(i)}=(1+r)g^{(i+1)} \]

and the share parameters satisfy

\[ \beta^{(i)} = \beta^{(n_p-1)} \frac{ (1+r)^{n_p-i} }{ r+2 }, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

Together with \(\sum_{i=1}^{n_p-1}\beta^{(i)}=1\), this determines the complete sequence of shares. Under the maintained decreasing-share assumption, the common-rate specification must also satisfy

\[ \beta^{(n_p-2)}>\beta^{(n_p-1)} \]

which, using the expression above, is equivalent to

\[ \frac{(1+r)^2}{r+2}>1 \]

or

\[ r>\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2} \]

For earlier periods, \(\beta^{(i)}/\beta^{(i+1)}=1+r>1\), so this terminal-adjacent condition is sufficient for the entire sequence to be strictly decreasing. In particular, when \(r=1\),

\[ \beta^{(i)} = \frac{ 2^{n_p-i} }{ 2^{n_p}-1 }, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

and

\[ \beta^{(n_p-1)} = \frac{3}{2^{n_p}-1} \]

which gives

\[ r^{(1)*}=\cdots=r^{(n_p-2)*}=1 \]

Numerical Example

Consider the four-period case, \(n_p=4\), with three production periods and

\[ \beta^{(1)}=\frac8{15}, \qquad \beta^{(2)}=\frac4{15}, \qquad \beta^{(3)}=\frac15 \]

and

\[ \alpha^{(1)}=\alpha^{(2)}=\alpha^{(3)}=1, \qquad \omega^{(1)}=\omega^{(2)}=\omega^{(3)}=100 \]

The recursive solution gives

\[ r^{(1)*}=r^{(2)*}=1, \qquad r^{(3)}=+\infty \]

Consumer income is

\[ w^*=375 \]

The terminal interest-inclusive product price is

\[ \tilde p^{(4)*} = \frac{\beta^{(3)}w^*}{\alpha^{(3)}\omega^{(3)}} = \frac34 \]

and the equilibrium commodity-price vector, interpreted in the limiting sense for the terminal product and labor prices, is

\[ \mathbf p^* = \left( 1,\frac12,0, \frac12,\frac14,0, 150,75,75 \right)^\top \]

Indeed,

\[ w^* = \frac12(100) + \frac14(100) + 150+75+75 = 375 \]

The equilibrium output and consumption quantities are

\[ y^{(i)*}=c^{(i+1)*}=100, \qquad i=1,2,3 \]

The firms’ money holdings are

\[ (h_f^{(1)*},h_f^{(2)*},h_f^{(3)*}) = \left( \frac13,\frac13,0 \right) \]

and the consumer’s money holdings are

\[ (h_c^{(1)*},h_c^{(2)*},h_c^{(3)*}) = \left( \frac23,\frac23,1 \right) \]

Hence, the money-service market clears in every period.

The equilibrium demand and supply matrices are

\[ \mathbf D^* = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&0&100\\ 0&0&0&100\\ 0&0&0&100\\ 100&0&0&0\\ 0&100&0&0\\ 0&0&100&0\\ \frac13&0&0&\frac23\\ 0&\frac13&0&\frac23\\ 0&0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf S^* = \begin{bmatrix} 100&0&0&0\\ 0&100&0&0\\ 0&0&100&0\\ 0&0&0&100\\ 0&0&0&100\\ 0&0&0&100\\ 0&0&0&1\\ 0&0&0&1\\ 0&0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Numerical Solution

The model can be solved using the GEMB framework for an arbitrary total number of periods \(n_p\). The variables \(r^{(1)},\ldots,r^{(n_p-2)}\) are determined jointly with commodity prices and agent activity levels. The terminal infinite interest rate is not introduced directly. Instead, the program uses the finite terminal interest-inclusive product price

\[ \tilde p^{(n_p)} = \lim_{r^{(n_p-1)}\rightarrow+\infty} (1+r^{(n_p-1)})p^{(n_p)} \]

A ConditionAgentSpec introduces \(r^{(1)},\ldots,r^{(n_p-2)}\) and \(\tilde p^{(n_p)}\) as endogenous variables. The interest-rate relation is

\[ r^{(i)} = \frac{ p_m^{(i)} }{ \displaystyle\sum_{j=i+1}^{n_p-1}p_m^{(j)} } = \frac{p_m^{(i)}}{v^{(i+1)}}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

where \(p_m^{(i)}\) is the current interest and \(v^{(i+1)}\) is the continuation value of money, which serves as the principal value for the period-\(i\) interest.

For numerical implementation, it can equivalently be written without division as

\[ r^{(i)} \sum_{j=i+1}^{n_p-1}p_m^{(j)} -p_m^{(i)} =0, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

For a nonterminal firm, an activity level \(y^{(i)}\) implies labor demand \(y^{(i)}/\alpha^{(i)}\) and money holdings

\[ h_f^{(i)} = \frac{ r^{(i)} p_l^{(i)}y^{(i)} }{ \alpha^{(i)} p_m^{(i)} }, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

The terminal firm is represented by the limiting form associated with \(r^{(n_p-1)}\rightarrow+\infty\), so its money holdings approach zero.

Given income \(w\), the consumer’s nonterminal product demand and money holdings are

\[ c^{(i+1)} = \frac{ \beta^{(i)}w }{ (1+r^{(i)})p^{(i+1)} } \]

and

\[ h_c^{(i)} = \frac{ r^{(i)}\beta^{(i)}w }{ (1+r^{(i)})p_m^{(i)} }, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_p-2 \]

The terminal demands are represented by the finite limiting expressions

\[ c^{(n_p)} = \frac{ \beta^{(n_p-1)}w }{ \tilde p^{(n_p)} } \]

and

\[ h_c^{(n_p-1)} = \frac{ \beta^{(n_p-1)}w }{ p_m^{(n_p-1)} } \]

The program can generate the common-rate share parameterization above for arbitrary total period count \(n_p\) and solve the resulting equilibrium system numerically. Under the maintained decreasing-share assumption, a common rate should satisfy \(r>(\sqrt5-1)/2\); the numerical example uses \(r=1\).

Infinite-Horizon Equilibrium

Consider an infinite-horizon version of the model, with production periods indexed by \(i=1,2,\ldots\). Production in period \(i\) uses labor \(i\) to produce product \(i+1\). There is no terminal production period and hence no terminal infinite-interest-rate condition.

Assume the following:

  1. The consumer has geometric expenditure shares

\[ \beta^{(i)}=(1-\rho)\rho^{i-1}, \qquad 0<\rho<1, \qquad i=1,2,\ldots \]

so that

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\beta^{(i)}=1 \]

  1. Productivity and labor endowment are constant over time:

\[ \alpha^{(i)}=\alpha>0, \qquad \omega^{(i)}=\omega>0 \]

  1. The consumer owns one unit of money, which supplies one unit of money service in every production period. Product 2 is the numeraire.

We seek an equilibrium with finite consumer income \(w^*>0\) and a summable sequence of interests. Define the money value by

\[ v^{(i)}=\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}p_m^{(j)*}, \qquad g^{(i)}=\frac{v^{(i)}}{w^*} \]

The interest-rate relation implies

\[ r^{(i)*}=\frac{p_m^{(i)*}}{v^{(i+1)}}, \qquad 1+r^{(i)*}=\frac{g^{(i)}}{g^{(i+1)}} \]

As in the finite-horizon model, money-service market clearing gives

\[ \frac{p_m^{(i)*}}{w^*} = \beta^{(i)} \frac{r^{(i)*}(r^{(i)*}+2)}{(1+r^{(i)*})^2} \]

For strictly positive interest rates, these relations yield

\[ \left(g^{(i)}\right)^2-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i)}-\beta^{(i)} g^{(i+1)}=0 \]

Since \(\beta^{(i+1)}=\rho\beta^{(i)}\), a proportional solution \(g^{(i)}=C\beta^{(i)}\) satisfies

\[ C=1+\rho \]

Thus,

\[ g^{(i)}=(1+\rho)\beta^{(i)} \]

and the equilibrium interest rate is constant:

\[ r^{(i)*}=\frac1\rho-1>0, \qquad i=1,2,\ldots \]

Equivalently, writing \(r=1/\rho-1\), the expenditure shares are

\[ \beta^{(i)}=\frac{r}{(1+r)^i} \]

The corresponding interests satisfy

\[ p_m^{(i)*} = w^*(g^{(i)}-g^{(i+1)}) = w^*(1-\rho^2)\beta^{(i)} \]

These interests are positive and summable. Moreover,

\[ \sum_{j=i}^{\infty}p_m^{(j)*} = w^*(1+\rho)\beta^{(i)} = w^*g^{(i)} \]

so the proposed sequence is consistent with the definition of money value, and \(v^{(i)}\rightarrow0\) as \(i\rightarrow\infty\).

Labor- and product-market clearing give constant equilibrium output and consumption:

\[ y^{(i)*}=c^{(i+1)*}=\alpha\omega \]

Taking product 2 as the numeraire yields

\[ w^*=\frac{\alpha\omega}{\rho(1-\rho)} \]

The equilibrium product and labor prices and the equilibrium interests are

\[ p^{(i+1)*}=\rho^{i-1}, \qquad p_l^{(i)*}=\alpha\rho^i, \qquad p_m^{(i)*} = \frac{1-\rho^2}{\rho}\alpha\omega\rho^{i-1} \]

They satisfy the consumer-income identity

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_l^{(i)*}\omega + \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_m^{(i)*} = \frac{\alpha\omega}{\rho(1-\rho)} = w^* \]

The equilibrium money holdings are also constant over time:

\[ h_f^{(i)*}=\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}, \qquad h_c^{(i)*}=\frac1{1+\rho} \]

Thus, the money-service market clears in every period. The infinite-horizon equilibrium has constant output, consumption, and money holdings, a constant interest rate determined by \(\rho\), and geometrically declining product prices, labor prices, and interests.

Steady State: A \(3\times2\) Equality-Form Wide-Sense Structural Equilibrium Model

The infinite-horizon steady state can be represented by a finite-dimensional equality-form wide-sense structural equilibrium model.

Assume the following:

  1. There are three commodity types: product, labor, and money service; and two economic-agent types: firm and consumer. Product is the numeraire.
  2. The consumer supplies \(\omega>0\) units of labor and owns one unit of money. The unit of money supplies one unit of money service in the steady state. The consumer consumes only product.
  3. The firm uses labor to produce product according to

\[ y=\alpha l, \qquad \alpha>0 \]

  1. Let \(\rho\in(0,1)\) denote the intertemporal discount factor. The steady-state interest rate is

\[ r=\frac1{\rho}-1 \]

Let

\[ \mathbf p=(1,p_l,p_m)^\top \]

where \(p_l\) is the labor price and \(p_m\) is the interest generated by one unit of money in the steady state. For a given output level \(y\), labor demand is

\[ l=\frac{y}{\alpha} \]

The firm and consumer must hold money to make transactions. Because one unit of money held supplies one unit of money service, their money holdings are represented as demands for the third commodity. Accordingly, the demand and supply matrices are

\[ \mathbf D(\mathbf p,y,c) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & c \\[6pt] \displaystyle\frac{y}{\alpha} & 0 \\[10pt] \displaystyle\frac{rp_l}{p_m}\frac{y}{\alpha} & \displaystyle\frac{rc}{p_m} \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf S(y) = \begin{bmatrix} y&0\\ 0&\omega\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

where the first and second columns correspond to the firm and consumer, respectively.

The steady-state equality-form wide-sense structural equilibrium model is

\[ \mathbf p^\top\mathbf D(\mathbf p,y,c) = \mathbf p^\top\mathbf S(y) \]

\[ \mathbf D(\mathbf p,y,c)\mathbf 1 = \mathbf S(y)\mathbf 1 \]

Solving the model gives

\[ l^*=\omega, \qquad y^*=c^*=\alpha\omega \]

and

\[ p_l^* = \rho\alpha \]

\[ p_m^* = \frac{1-\rho^2}{\rho}\alpha\omega \]

The corresponding equilibrium money holdings are

\[ h_f^* = \frac{\rho}{1+\rho}, \qquad h_c^* = \frac1{1+\rho} \]

so that

\[ h_f^*+h_c^*=1 \]
